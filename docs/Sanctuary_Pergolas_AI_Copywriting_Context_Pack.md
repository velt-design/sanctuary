# Sanctuary Pergolas — AI Copywriting Context Pack

## Purpose and Evidence Standard

Use this document as the standing strategic context for writing Sanctuary Pergolas website pages, landing pages, Google Ads, Meta Ads, brochures, proposals, follow-up emails and sales copy.

The source material contains structured records of enquiries, sales conversations, customer pain points, objections, intent signals, quote-stage wording, outcomes and claim-review flags. The guidance below gives greatest weight to:

1. repeated patterns across many conversations;
2. high-confidence, public-safe customer language;
3. messages followed by an explicit step forward, such as a site visit, design revision, quote acceptance, deposit or confirmed scheduling; and
4. wording marked as stronger or more reusable in the source.

Progression after a message is an **association, not proof that the wording caused conversion**. Treat the phrase bank and sales patterns as informed hypotheses to test, not guaranteed formulas.

Never expose or reconstruct customer names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, quote numbers, private project details, internal records or source locations. Customer wording in this pack is for language modelling and message inspiration. Do not present it as a testimonial or attribute it to a real customer without documented permission.

The source does not contain a formal legal claim-approval register. In this pack, **approved** means low-risk, well-supported language suitable for ordinary marketing when it remains accurate. It does not mean legal, regulatory or technical sign-off.

## 1. Strategic North Star

### Recommended positioning statement

**Sanctuary Pergolas designs and installs custom, design-led pergolas that make outdoor areas usable more often—adding shelter while preserving light, openness and the character of the home.**

### Compressed message

**Cover the deck. Keep the light. Make it feel part of the home.**

This is the clearest synthesis of recurring customer demand. Customers repeatedly want to cover a deck or outdoor area, but they do not want the result to feel dark, boxed in, visually awkward or detached from the architecture.

### Category and premium stance

Sanctuary should be positioned as a **custom pergola design-and-installation specialist**, not a commodity roof supplier, generic kitset seller or cheapest-price operator.

The premium is justified through the total outcome:

- a design developed around the house, deck, roofline and way the space will be used;
- a cleaner, more considered fit than an off-the-shelf structure;
- options that balance shelter, light, heat, privacy, views and openness;
- a guided process from site information through design, quote, refinement and installation; and
- proof that reduces risk: drawings or renders, photos of comparable projects, clear inclusions, documented specifications and verified warranty information.

Do not lead with material thickness, technical specifications or the word “premium” alone. Lead with the life and design outcome, then substantiate it.

### The customer’s real purchase

Customers are buying five things at once:

1. **More usable outdoor living:** a deck or patio they can use more often despite rain, sun or wind.
2. **A design that belongs:** a structure that suits the house rather than looking added on.
3. **Light without exposure:** cover and comfort without sacrificing natural light, sky views or brightness inside.
4. **Confidence before commitment:** clarity about appearance, materials, inclusions, price drivers, timing and next steps.
5. **A finished result worth looking at:** a clean, substantial, well-resolved addition rather than a merely functional cover.

## 2. Positioning and Value Proposition Hierarchy

### Tier 1: More usable outdoor living

This is the broadest and most frequently expressed customer need. Customers talk about covering a deck, creating an outdoor room, entertaining, leaving doors open, fitting a table and barbecue, and using the area “all year round”.

**Core angle:** Make an underused deck a sheltered extension of everyday living.

**Useful language:**

- Use your deck more often.
- Create a covered outdoor area for everyday living and entertaining.
- Add shelter where rain, strong sun or wind currently limits the space.
- Turn an exposed deck into a more comfortable, usable outdoor room.

Avoid promising perfect year-round comfort or complete weather exclusion unless the exact design supports that claim.

### Tier 1: Custom fit and architectural integration

A large share of serious enquiries concern how the pergola meets the house: roofline, posts, existing timber, gutters, flashings, fascia, colour, sightlines and visual balance. Customers ask to remove or minimise posts, match existing joinery, keep a traditional or modern house style, and see what the finished design will look like.

**Core angle:** Designed for this home, not selected from a generic template.

**Useful language:**

- Custom-designed around your home, deck and roofline.
- Made to keep the area as open and uncluttered as the site allows.
- Planned to complement the house from both the deck and the street.
- Colour, roof form, post placement and finishing details considered together.

Do not imply that every site can achieve post-free spans, a particular roof connection or any requested form. Use “subject to site conditions, design and engineering” where relevant.

### Tier 1: Shelter without sacrificing light

Customers commonly fear that a new roof will darken the lounge, block sunlight, remove the view of the sky or create a hot “glasshouse” effect. This is not a secondary technical question; it is a central buying tension.

**Core angle:** Add cover while protecting the bright, open quality of the home.

**Useful language:**

- Get more shelter without making the space feel closed in.
- Keep natural light moving into the house.
- Compare clear and tinted roof options around light, glare, appearance and maintenance.
- Design the cover around the level of openness and shade you want.

Avoid quantified UV, heat or light-performance figures until the exact product, tint and approved technical source are confirmed.

### Tier 2: Premium design and finish

Customers who progress often care about visual weight, clean lines, matching colours, discreet fixings, pane layout, roof style and a result that looks substantial. “Premium” works when translated into visible decisions.

**Core angle:** A considered addition with a clean, resolved finish.

Prove this with project photography, close-up details, drawings, material samples, finish options and specific explanations. Replace vague claims such as “superior craftsmanship” with concrete evidence.

### Tier 2: A clear, consultative path to the right design

Positive progression is repeatedly associated with simple next steps, visual clarification, honest trade-off explanations and precise confirmation of what happens next.

**Core angle:** Sanctuary helps the customer make a confident decision, not merely choose a product.

The mechanism is:

**share details → assess the site → compare options → confirm design and scope → quote clearly → refine if needed → schedule after acceptance**.

### Competitive frame

Customers compare Sanctuary with louvres, glass, corrugated roofing, other acrylic systems, cheaper quotes and kitset-style options. Do not declare one technology universally better. Frame the decision around the outcome:

- How much weather protection is required?
- How important are natural light and sky views?
- How should the structure relate to the house?
- Are blinds, privacy or enclosure needed now or later?
- What is included in each quote?
- What site, drainage, consent or engineering work may be required?

Sanctuary should win through **fit, clarity, design judgement and proof**, not competitor criticism.

## 3. Ideal Customer Profiles

### Primary ICP 1: The underused-deck homeowner

**Situation:** Has an existing deck, patio or outdoor area that is exposed to rain, strong sun, wind or seasonal discomfort.

**Desired outcome:** A covered area for meals, entertaining, children, pets, relaxing or leaving doors open more often.

**Typical language:** “cover our deck”, “use it all year round”, “shelter from sun and rain”, “usable when it’s raining”, “more weather protection”.

**Buying triggers:** A wet season, summer heat, an upcoming event, a renovation, new outdoor furniture, a growing family or frustration that a large deck is rarely used.

**Main objections:** Price, whether the cover will keep enough light, whether rain will still enter from the side, and whether blinds are needed.

**Best message:** Lead with increased use of the space; then show how roof and blind options balance shelter, light and openness.

### Primary ICP 2: The design-conscious renovator

**Situation:** Owns a home where architectural fit matters. May be renovating, building, replacing an old structure or resolving a difficult roofline.

**Desired outcome:** A pergola that looks intentional and visually integrated, with clean sightlines and considered colours and details.

**Typical language:** “fit in with the house”, “colour match the existing joinery”, “look more balanced on the façade”, “no posts in the middle”, “leave it open as much as possible”, “what will it look like?”

**Buying triggers:** Exterior renovation, new deck, landscaping, replacement of an unattractive timber or ageing cover, or seeing a Sanctuary project with a similar house style.

**Main objections:** Design ambiguity, post placement, roof connection, drainage, pane layout, colour choice and the fear of making the home darker or less attractive.

**Best message:** Emphasise site-specific design, visualisation, comparable project examples and refinement before final commitment.

### Primary ICP 3: The premium buyer comparing quotes

**Situation:** Has budget and intent, but is comparing providers, systems or configurations. May see Sanctuary as more expensive and needs to understand the difference.

**Desired outcome:** Confidence that the selected solution will deliver the right appearance, fit, inclusions and long-term value.

**Typical language:** “what exactly is included?”, “just want to make sure we’ve covered everything”, “we can’t decide until we compare the options”, “do the costs remain the same?”, “we can’t buy totally unseen”.

**Buying triggers:** Receipt of multiple quotes, a recommendation, viewing a completed project, a site visit or a design that finally resolves the home-specific problem.

**Main objections:** Price, unclear scope, warranty, materials, proof of similar work and whether apparently similar quotes are genuinely comparable.

**Best message:** Compare scope and outcomes line by line. Explain price drivers without defensiveness. Use visual proof, written inclusions and verified warranty documents.

### Secondary ICP 4: The complex-site or compliance-conscious homeowner

**Situation:** Has a high-wind or exposed site, unusual geometry, boundary constraints, body-corporate requirements, a builder or architect involved, or uncertainty about consent and drainage.

**Desired outcome:** A feasible, well-documented solution with fewer surprises.

**Main objections:** Structural suitability, spans, fixings, water flow, house penetration, council requirements and responsibility for documentation.

**Best message:** Calm competence and conditional precision. Explain what is known, what must be checked, who is responsible and the next evidence required. Never use blanket consent, wind or engineering claims.

### Secondary ICP 5: The replacement or upgrade buyer

**Situation:** Has an ageing, cracked, rusting, leaking, unattractive or outdated pergola or canopy.

**Desired outcome:** A cleaner, more modern structure that better suits the current home and outdoor use.

**Main objections:** Removal, compatibility with existing structures, cost, weather performance and whether repair or full replacement is more sensible.

**Best message:** Show before-and-after transformations and explain why the proposed scope is a repair, partial upgrade or replacement.

### Lower-fit or qualification-heavy enquiries

Lower progression is associated with enquiries that are outside the verified service area, seek DIY or kitset supply only, ask only for the cheapest option, provide no location or project detail, or request a rough price without engaging in design or site questions.

Copy and forms should politely qualify these leads by requesting:

- service location;
- photos of the house and area;
- rough width and projection;
- desired use and main weather problem;
- preferred style or examples;
- timing; and
- whether the customer wants design and installation or supply only.

Do not make the form so long that it blocks serious early-stage buyers. Photos, location and rough dimensions are the most useful first filters.

## 4. Motivations, Pain Points and Buying Triggers

Prioritise these customer jobs in roughly this order:

| Customer job | Underlying tension | Copy implication |
|---|---|---|
| Use the deck more often | Valuable space is exposed or underused | Lead with everyday use, not product specifications |
| Get rain and wind protection | Weather interrupts meals, access and entertaining | Show the covered result and optional side protection; avoid absolute waterproof promises |
| Add shade without excessive heat or darkness | The customer wants comfort but fears a closed-in result | Present light, tint, shade and airflow as design trade-offs |
| Make the pergola suit the house | Generic structures may look awkward | Show similar house styles, roofline decisions and colour matching |
| Keep the area open | Posts, low beams and narrow panes can spoil views and circulation | Discuss clean sightlines and site-dependent post placement |
| Understand price and value | Quotes vary and inclusions are hard to compare | Explain assumptions, inclusions, exclusions and cost drivers clearly |
| Reduce project risk | Consent, drainage, durability and installation are unfamiliar | Use written proof, process clarity and conditional expert guidance |
| Finish before a deadline | Summer, Christmas, events or other works create urgency | State current timing honestly and use only real capacity constraints |

### Strong intent signals

Treat these as signs to move from education to a concrete next step:

- asks about deposit, acceptance or how to proceed;
- asks about installation timing or a specific deadline;
- follows up proactively;
- requests or accepts a site visit;
- provides address, photos, plans or useful dimensions;
- asks detailed questions about design, house connection, materials or inclusions;
- requests a revised quote or chooses an option;
- involves a partner, builder, architect or body corporate; or
- says “we’d like to go ahead”, “keen to proceed” or “what are the next steps?”

Do not mistake early measurements or a quote request alone for a guaranteed high-value lead. Use them to invite the next commitment.

## 5. Objection Strategy and Evidence-Based Responses

| Objection | What the customer is really asking | Best response pattern | Best evidence | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **“The price is too high.”** | Is the difference worth it, and can the outcome fit my budget? | Acknowledge the constraint. Explain the specific cost drivers—size, bay count, roof form, blinds, access, drainage or complexity. Offer a revised dimension, staged extra or alternative configuration without stripping out the core outcome. | Side-by-side scope, drawings, current itemised quote, comparable project | Discounting immediately; “you get what you pay for”; vague quality claims |
| **“I’m comparing quotes.”** | Are these genuinely like for like? | Re-state exactly what Sanctuary includes and excludes. Explain material, configuration and installation differences in plain language. Invite the customer to compare the finished outcome, not only the headline total. | Inclusion checklist, option comparison, photos, verified warranty | Attacking competitors; unsupported superiority claims |
| **“I can’t picture the design.”** | Will it look right and work with my home? | Show a render, drawing, marked-up photo or similar completed home. Explain roofline, posts, colour, infills and drainage. Offer a revision or site meeting around the unresolved point. | Visuals, samples, before/after projects | “Trust us”; forcing acceptance before design clarity |
| **“Will it make the house dark or too hot?”** | Am I solving one problem by creating another? | Acknowledge the trade-off. Compare clear and tinted options around natural light, glare, appearance, dirt visibility and desired shade. Relate the decision to room orientation and how the area is used. | Approved product data, physical samples, relevant installed examples | Unverified UV/heat percentages; pretending one tint is universally best |
| **“What material should I choose?”** | I do not understand the practical differences. | Translate each option into visible and everyday consequences. Use a simple comparison and recommend based on the customer’s priorities. | Current specifications, samples, project photos | Jargon dumps; calling acrylic “glass”; unsupported lifespan claims |
| **“Will it leak or handle the weather?”** | Can I trust the result in real conditions? | Clarify the intended level of protection and the limits created by open sides, existing gutters, house connections and consent restrictions. Explain drainage and flashing decisions. Assess the site rather than guaranteeing a universal outcome. | Site assessment, drawings, drainage details, approved warranty, service process | “Waterproof”, “leak-free” or “built for any weather” without substantiation |
| **“Do I need consent?”** | Will this create cost, delay or compliance risk? | Say that requirements depend on the complete design and site, not one dimension alone. Explain what can be assessed now, what must be confirmed and what documentation Sanctuary can supply or coordinate. | Current regulatory advice, plans, engineering and council documentation | Blanket “under X m² means no consent” claims; legal certainty without review |
| **“Is it strong enough for this site or span?”** | Can I avoid posts without compromising safety? | Explain that post placement, spans and fixings are site- and design-dependent. Confirm only what the reviewed design or engineering supports. Offer the cleanest feasible alternative. | Site measurements, engineering, approved span tables, fixing details | Universal wind-speed, wind-zone or maximum-span claims |
| **“What does the warranty cover?”** | What happens if something fails? | Provide the current written warranty and separate Sanctuary workmanship coverage from any manufacturer product warranty. State duration, coverage, exclusions and claim process exactly. | Current signed-off warranty documents | Combining different warranties; using historical terms from old quotes |
| **“We’re not ready / need partner approval.”** | Help us decide without pressure. | Give a concise comparison, visuals and a clear re-entry point. Confirm which details can remain flexible and what would need to be decided before scheduling. | One-page option summary, similar projects, current quote validity | Repeated pressure, false scarcity, treating delay as rejection |

The most productive objection responses are not clever rebuttals. They **reduce uncertainty with a fact, a visual, a design change or a clear next step**.

## 7. Progression-Associated Messaging

The source most consistently links progress with messages that are direct, specific and easy to act on. The useful pattern is **clarity → confidence → one next step**.

### First response

Answer the main question immediately. Then request the minimum information needed to advance.

**Recommended pattern:**

> Yes, that type of pergola is within our scope. To give you a useful first indication, please send the location, a few photos and the rough width and projection of the area.

### Site-visit invitation

Explain why the visit matters and make booking simple.

> A site visit is the next step so we can confirm the roof connection, post positions and the most accurate design and quote. Send your address and phone number and we’ll arrange a suitable time.

Only call the visit free when the current fee and service area have been verified.

### Design reassurance

Name the exact design concern and show how it will be resolved.

> We can revise the layout to keep the area as open as possible. We’ll confirm whether that post can be removed once the roof structure and span have been checked.

### Option or material explanation

Use a small number of decision criteria, not a technical lecture.

> The main choice is between maximum natural light and a little more tint and glare control. We’ll show you both options on comparable projects so you can judge the look as well as the specification.

### Quote presentation

A strong quote message should state:

- the agreed design and dimensions;
- what is included and excluded;
- optional items and their effect on price;
- assumptions that could change the quote;
- current timing; and
- the exact next step.

> Attached is the revised design and quote. It includes the pergola structure, roofing, guttering and installation; the blinds are shown as a separate option. The wider version adds another structural bay, which is the main reason for the price difference. Let us know which option you prefer and we’ll confirm the next step.

### Commitment and scheduling

Progression-associated messages make the transaction trigger explicit and confirm receipt quickly.

> Once the agreed deposit is received, we’ll add the project to the schedule and confirm what happens next.

> We’ve received your deposit and your project is now booked in. We’ll update you with the installation window and any final design decisions still required.

Deposit percentage, balance timing and scheduling policy must be current before use.

### Follow-up and reactivation

Use a low-pressure status question and give the customer a useful reason to respond.

> Just checking whether you’re still planning to go ahead, or whether you’d like us to update the design, price or timing first.

> Once the deck work is complete, let us know and we can pick the project up from there.

### Real urgency only

Capacity language can prompt action when it is true:

> We have limited installation capacity remaining before **[verified deadline]**. To secure a place, the design and deposit would need to be confirmed by **[verified date]**.

Never manufacture scarcity or recycle an old lead-time statement.

## 8. Tone, Vocabulary and Writing Principles

### Brand voice

Sanctuary should sound like a **calm, practical design specialist**: confident without swagger, premium without pretension, technically capable without jargon, and friendly without becoming casual or vague.

Use New Zealand English: “colour”, “metres”, “organise”, “custom-designed”, “building consent”, “quote” and “GST”.

### Preferred vocabulary

Use frequently:

- custom-designed, tailored, designed around your home;
- deck, outdoor area, outdoor room, cover, shelter;
- open, bright, natural light, sightlines;
- roofline, post placement, house connection, colour match;
- options, compare, recommend, confirm;
- included, excluded, scope, assumptions;
- site visit, design, quote, revision, next step, schedule;
- similar completed projects, drawings, renders, samples.

Use carefully and only with proof:

- premium, engineered, weather-tight, durable, low-maintenance;
- UV protection, heat reduction, wind rating, maximum span;
- warranty, waterproof, consent-exempt;
- free, fastest, best, leading or guaranteed.

### Writing rules

1. **Lead with the customer’s desired change.** Product and material details come second.
2. **Name the trade-off honestly.** Light versus shade, openness versus enclosure, span versus supports, size versus budget.
3. **Use specific nouns and verbs.** “Confirm the roof connection” is stronger than “explore your vision”.
4. **Give one primary next step.** Do not end with several competing calls to action.
5. **Reduce ambiguity.** State what is included, what depends on site review and what the customer needs to supply.
6. **Use proof beside the claim.** Pair “designed to fit the home” with a drawing, project image or design explanation.
7. **Keep sentences short.** Especially in ads, forms, quote emails and objection responses.
8. **Write for a considered purchase.** Do not force artificial urgency or impulse-buy language.
9. **Treat “premium” as an outcome.** Show cleaner integration, clearer scope and a better-resolved finish.
10. **Use technical detail only when it helps a decision.** Translate every specification into its practical consequence.

### CTA hierarchy

**Early stage:** Send photos and rough dimensions; explore design options; request a site visit.

**Mid stage:** Request a tailored quote; compare the options; review a similar project; confirm the preferred design.

**Late stage:** Approve the revised quote; confirm the deposit; secure a place in the schedule.

Prefer these over “Learn more”, “Enquire now” or “Contact us”.

## 9. Claim Governance

### Approved for general qualitative use

These claim families are strongly supported and relatively low-risk when accurately phrased:

- Sanctuary designs and installs custom pergolas.
- Designs are developed around the home, deck, desired use and site conditions.
- Customers can be shown options, drawings, renders or examples where available.
- Pergolas can be designed to add shelter while considering natural light and openness.
- Options may include different roof forms, tints, blinds or side protection where currently offered.
- A site assessment can help confirm design, scope and price.
- Quotes should explain inclusions, exclusions and options before the customer proceeds.
- Sanctuary can share comparable completed projects when image permissions and relevance are confirmed.

Phrase benefits as intent or outcome, not absolutes: “designed to keep the area bright” is safer than “will not reduce indoor light”.

### Conditionally usable after current operational verification

Check these on every campaign or page update:

- current service area;
- whether site visits are available and whether they are free in that location;
- current lead time and installation availability;
- deposit percentage, balance terms and quote-validity period;
- current price ranges or starting prices;
- current roof, tint, blind, lighting and colour options;
- whether Sanctuary supplies only, installs only, repairs or removes old structures;
- current consent, engineering or documentation services;
- current material brands, thicknesses, coatings and component dimensions.

Use a placeholder rather than guessing: `[[VERIFY: current lead time]]`.

### Requires written technical, legal or supplier substantiation

Do not publish these without an approved source matched to the exact product and design:

- warranty duration, scope, exclusions and whether it covers materials, workmanship, electrical work, coatings or manufacturer products;
- exact UV-blocking, heat-reduction or light-transmission percentages;
- wind-speed ratings, suitability for every wind zone or “NZ conditions” claims;
- maximum unsupported spans or claims that posts can be removed;
- waterproof, leak-free, fully weather-tight or “dry in all weather” promises;
- product lifespan, sealant life, maintenance intervals or “maintenance-free” claims;
- structural, engineering, fire, code or compliance claims;
- blanket statements that a pergola below a particular area does not require consent;
- direct product superiority or performance comparisons;
- historical prices, quote examples and old installation timeframes.

### Testimonials and project proof

Customer praise, reviews and finished-project photos can be powerful, but only use them when:

- permission is documented;
- the wording is reproduced accurately;
- identifying details are intentionally approved;
- the result is not presented as typical without evidence; and
- any performance statement inside the testimonial is independently supportable.

Anonymous customer-language research is not permission to publish a testimonial.

## 10. Language and Positioning to Avoid

Avoid generic outdoor-living clichés unless a brief deliberately calls for them:

- “transform your backyard”
- “create your own private sanctuary”
- “the ultimate outdoor oasis”
- “seamless indoor-outdoor flow”
- “entertain in style”
- “bring your dream to life”
- “where luxury meets functionality”
- “built to withstand anything”
- “unmatched quality” or “best in class”

Avoid positioning Sanctuary as:

- the cheapest or most affordable option;
- a kitset, materials-only or one-size-fits-all supplier;
- a generic “luxury lifestyle” brand with no design substance;
- universally better than louvres, glass or every competing system;
- able to solve every site without assessment;
- certain about council, engineering, wind or waterproofing from a photo alone.

Do not overuse “premium”. Do not call acrylic “glass”. Do not promise “no posts”, “no leaks”, “no maintenance”, “no consent” or a fixed installation time without verification.

## 11. Channel-Specific Guidance

### Website and landing pages

The website should move from emotional relevance to practical certainty.

**Recommended page sequence:**

1. **Hero:** one customer outcome, one differentiator and one primary CTA.
2. **Problem recognition:** rain, sun, wind, underused deck, darkness or poor integration.
3. **Three message pillars:** more usable space; designed to belong; shelter without losing light.
4. **Project proof:** comparable homes, before/after images, close-up finish details and approved reviews.
5. **How it works:** details/photos, site visit, design, quote, refinement, acceptance, scheduling and installation.
6. **Options and trade-offs:** roof form, tint, blinds, openness, light, privacy and price drivers.
7. **FAQ:** price, inclusions, timing, site visits, light, heat, rain, consent, warranty, maintenance and service area.
8. **Qualification CTA:** request location, photos, rough dimensions, desired outcome and timing.

**Hero direction:**

> **Cover the deck. Keep the light.**  
> Custom pergolas designed to add shelter, suit your home and make the outdoor area usable more often.  
> **CTA: Request a site visit**

Alternative:

> **A pergola should look like it belongs on the house.**  
> Sanctuary designs and installs tailored pergolas around your roofline, outdoor space and the way you want to use it.  
> **CTA: Get a tailored quote**

Use one hero idea at a time. Do not stack every feature into the opening paragraph.

Create dedicated pages for high-intent needs where relevant: custom pergolas, covered decks, pitched or gable pergolas, pergolas with blinds, replacement pergolas, light-preserving roof options and local service pages. Each page must use only currently offered products and verified locations.

Landing-page message must mirror the ad group. A “pergola with blinds” ad should land on a page that immediately shows blinds, weather/privacy use cases, options, proof and the correct CTA.

Use current price or “free site visit” language only in short-lived ads tied to verified terms. Consider negative keywords for DIY, kitset, wholesale materials, free plans, cheapest-only searches and locations outside the service area—only where Sanctuary does not serve those needs.

### Meta Ads

Meta is interruption-based. Lead with a recognisable frustration or a visual contrast, not “we build pergolas”.

**Best creative territories:**

1. **Rain to usable room:** exposed deck versus sheltered everyday use.
2. **Cover without darkness:** exterior pergola plus interior view showing retained brightness.
3. **Designed to belong:** before/after façade, roofline and colour integration.
4. **Open sightlines:** thoughtful post placement and uncluttered space, with appropriate site-dependent wording.
5. **Blinds and side protection:** wind, rain, privacy and evening use.
6. **Replacement transformation:** ageing structure replaced by a cleaner design.

**Hook bank:**

- Love the deck, but not the rain?
- Want cover without making the lounge feel dark?
- Your pergola should look like part of the house—not an afterthought.
- Keep the outdoor area open, bright and usable more often.
- Replacing an old pergola? Start with the way you want the space to work.
- Not sure whether clear, tinted or blinds will suit? Compare the options before you commit.

**Recommended cold-audience structure:**

> **Hook:** Name one frustration in customer language.  
> **Outcome:** Show the brighter, sheltered, better-integrated result.  
> **Mechanism:** Custom design around the home and site.  
> **Proof:** Similar completed project, drawing or approved review.  
> **CTA:** Send photos and rough dimensions / request a site visit.

For retargeting, move from inspiration to certainty: show quote inclusions, design revisions, option comparisons, the process, verified warranty and current scheduling information.

Lead forms should capture service location, photos, rough dimensions, desired use, main concern and timeframe. The thank-you screen should state the next step precisely rather than saying only “we’ll be in touch”.

Do not use quantified performance, warranty, consent, wind or waterproof claims in creative unless that exact claim has current written approval.

### Brochures, proposals and sales copy

Brochures should be visually led and organised around customer decisions, not a long company history. Use this order: desired outcome, project examples, design choices, process, proof, claim-approved specifications and next step.

Sales emails should answer the customer’s main question in the first sentence, explain one or two relevant trade-offs, and end with a single action. Proposal cover notes should make scope and differences between options unmistakable.
